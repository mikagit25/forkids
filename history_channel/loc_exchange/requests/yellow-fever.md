> Through the nineteenth century, yellow fever struck again and again, from New Orleans to Rio de Janeiro. Most doctors believed it spread through filth and miasma, or through the clothes and bedding of the sick. Cities burned tar in the streets and fumigated houses. In 1881, a Cuban physician named Carlos Finlay proposed a different idea. He argued that yellow fever was carried by a mosquito. For almost twenty years, few people took him seriously.

## scene `vaccine` — IX. The Vaccine 1937
wanted: up to 3 photos
searches: Max Theiler, Rockefeller Foundation laboratory 1930s

> Mosquito control worked in cities, but yellow fever also lived in monkeys in the jungles of Africa and South America, so it could never be wiped out that way. In 1937, the South African virologist Max Theiler, working at the Rockefeller Foundation in New York, developed a vaccine called 17D. A single dose gives protection for life. It is still used today. Theiler received the Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in 1951.

> Yellow fever has not been defeated. It still circulates in parts of Africa and South America, and causes tens of thousands of deaths in some years, mostly where vaccination coverage is low. Travellers to these regions are often required to show proof of vaccination, the famous yellow card.
