> Smallpox shaped history. When Europeans arrived in the Americas, they brought smallpox with them. Indigenous peoples had never encountered it, and epidemics devastated the Aztec and Inca empires, helping a few hundred Spanish soldiers conquer them. In Europe, it struck royal families as easily as anyone. It killed a king of France, Louis the Fifteenth, in 1774, and an emperor of Austria, and changed the line of succession in several countries. In eighteenth-century Europe, it is estimated to have killed around four hundred thousand people every year.

## scene `balmis` — VI. A Living Chain 1803

> There was a problem. Without refrigeration, the vaccine could not survive long sea voyages. In 1803, the Spanish Crown found an extraordinary solution. A doctor named Francisco Javier de Balmis sailed for the Americas with a group of orphan boys. Before departure, two boys were vaccinated. As the sores developed, material from them was used to vaccinate the next two, and so on, keeping the vaccine alive, child to child, across the Atlantic. The expedition carried smallpox vaccination to Spanish America and on to the Philippines.

## scene `last-death` — IX. The Last Victim 1978
wanted: up to 3 photos

> There was one more tragedy. In 1978, a medical photographer named Janet Parker, who worked at Birmingham University Medical School in England, caught smallpox from a laboratory in the building where the virus was being studied. She died, the last person known to have been killed by smallpox. On the eighth of May 1980, the World Health Assembly declared the world free of smallpox. It was the first, and is still the only, human disease ever eradicated.
